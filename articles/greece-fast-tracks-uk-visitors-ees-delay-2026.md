## ARTICLE BODY

Every non-EU traveler who has flown into Athens, Corfu, or Heraklion this summer has lived through some version of the same story: a longer-than-expected wait at passport control, a first-time fingerprint scan, a slightly confused sense of whether the process actually applied to them. Behind that individual experience sits one of the more chaotic policy episodes in recent EU border history — and Greece, more than almost any other Schengen country, spent 2026 at the center of it.

The short version: the EU's new Entry/Exit System (EES) went fully live on April 10, 2026, requiring biometric registration — fingerprints and a facial scan — from non-EU travelers at every external Schengen border. ## What EES actually is — and why Greece matters disproportionately

The Entry/Exit System is an automated IT system, administered by the EU agency eu-LISA, that electronically records every entry and exit of non-EU, non-EEA nationals at the external borders of the Schengen Area's 29 participating countries. It replaces the manual passport stamp — the ink-and-rubber process that has defined European land, air, and sea border crossings for decades — with a digital record built from four fingerprints (right hand) and a facial photograph, captured on a traveler's first crossing and reused, with a quick face-match, on every crossing for three years thereafter. Children under 12 are exempt from fingerprinting. Registration happens entirely at the border; there is no advance application, and — unlike the separate ETIAS system still to come — no fee.

The rollout was phased: gradual introduction began October 12, 2025, with individual border points bringing EES online at their own pace, before the system became mandatory everywhere on April 10, 2026. Ireland and Cyprus, though EU members, sit outside the Schengen Area and do not participate.

Greece is disproportionately exposed to how well or badly this system runs, for a simple structural reason: tourism drives roughly 30% of Greek GDP directly and indirectly, according to WTTC and Greek tourism-authority estimates, and a substantial share of that tourism arrives from outside the EU. Bank of Greece figures put 2025 international arrivals at 37.98 million, with non-EU visitors growing 10% year-on-year against 2.8% EU growth — meaning the fastest-growing part of Greece's visitor base is precisely the population EES applies to. The United Kingdom alone sent [4.89 million visitors to Greece in 2025](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-tourism-source-markets), more than any other single market, non-EU or otherwise, spending €3.74 billion. American visitors added another 1.55 million at the highest per-trip spend of any major market. Athens, Heraklion, Rhodes, Corfu, Santorini, and Mykonos all function as high-volume non-EU entry points during peak summer — precisely when EES processing time is most likely to compound into real queues.

That combination — a huge, fast-growing non-EU visitor base concentrated into a short, intense summer season, arriving through island airports never designed for biometric-registration throughput — is the underlying reason Greece became the most consequential test case for how EES would actually work in practice, rather than on paper.


## The exemption: what Greece actually did, and why it couldn't last

In the first days after April 10, individual friction points emerged quickly — Corfu Airport in particular saw congestion build as biometric registration slowed processing during a high-volume arrival window. Rather than wait for a coordinated EU response, Greek authorities made a unilateral call: British passport holders would not have their biometrics collected at all. The Greek Embassy in London confirmed the policy directly, stating that "British passport holders are excluded from biometric registration at Greek border crossing points" as of April 10. Eleni Skarveli, director of the Greek National Tourism Organisation in the UK, framed the move as designed to deliver a smoother arrival experience and cut waiting times — a description that tracked closely with UK Foreign Office guidance at the time, which told British travelers that Greek authorities would not be collecting their biometric data under the new system.

The practical case for the exemption was straightforward. British travelers are Greece's single largest non-EU market by a wide margin — outnumbering every other non-European nationality visiting Greece combined, according to Greek tourism officials at the time — and some Corfu, Crete, and Rhodes airports handle more than 2,000 UK arrivals and departures on peak summer days. Manual passport stamping takes roughly 15 seconds per traveler; first-time biometric registration takes several minutes. At UK volumes, the arithmetic difference translates into hours of queue time across a single peak day.

Tourism Minister Olga Kefalogianni became the public face of the policy, and her framing evolved carefully over successive statements through April and May. She characterized the change not as a legal exemption but as a "facilitation" — telling reporters Greece was not breaching EU rules, which do permit temporary biometric suspensions at specific crossing points during periods of congestion, but do not permit a blanket carve-out for one nationality. By May 11, speaking to The Independent, she went further, committing to make the arrangement last for the entire summer rather than treating it as a stopgap: "We aim to actually make sure that this facilitation is not just valid for this year." She described the promised UK experience in strikingly concrete terms — entry and exit taking "a minute or so," "less than a couple of minutes" — a direct, repeated contrast with the multi-hour queues being reported elsewhere in Europe.

The European Commission's objection was immediate and specific, resting on the actual legal text of the EES regulation rather than a general preference for uniformity. Under the rules, member states may briefly pause biometric collection at a specific border crossing point when queues become excessive — a location-based, congestion-triggered mechanism. What they cannot do is exempt an entire nationality as a standing policy, because doing so creates a structural data-integrity problem: a traveler who enters through a biometric-collecting border and exits through a non-collecting one (or vice versa) risks being incorrectly flagged by the EES database as having overstayed their 90-day limit, since the system would have no exit record to match against their entry record. Commission officials confirmed they were in direct contact with Athens "to clarify the situation and recall the existing rules," and noted publicly that Portugal and Italy — despite unconfirmed reports they might follow Greece's lead — had no plans to introduce equivalent nationality-based exemptions of their own.

Greece held its position publicly through mid-May regardless, with border staff continuing to stamp UK passports by hand. That changed on May 29, 2026, when the Greek Foreign Ministry issued a reversal, stating it had no information indicating that "specific nationalities are temporarily exempt from the relevant procedure." The policy affected more than symbolism: from that date forward, every non-EU traveler passing through Greek biometric scanners — British or otherwise — became subject to identical EES registration, and Greek police adopted a more procedural public posture, describing the system as "fully operational" and committing to use only the congestion-based suspension tools available under EU law, applied by location rather than by nationality.

The reversal closed out the only large-scale, sustained nationality exemption attempted anywhere in the Schengen Area during EES's 2026 rollout. It is worth being precise about what remains true afterward: individual border crossing points in Greece — like those in every other Schengen country — retain the ability to pause biometric collection temporarily during severe congestion, under the general flexibility mechanism discussed below. What ended on May 29 was Greece's attempt to apply that flexibility as a standing, nationality-wide policy rather than a location-specific, time-limited one.


## Did the exemption actually help bookings? The mixed evidence

One detail complicates the narrative of a policy that simply failed: UK booking data for Greece held up, and by some measures improved, during the exemption period. Ministry of Tourism figures cited in industry press showed a 2.8% rise in UK bookings for June 2026 following the initial announcement, and Greece's broader 2025 season — the year immediately preceding this saga — had already delivered record UK spending of €3.74 billion, up 18.5% year-on-year. Whether the exemption meaningfully drove incremental June bookings, or whether it simply coincided with an already-strong UK-to-Greece booking trend that has held for three consecutive years, is not something the available data can cleanly separate. What is clear is that the exemption's political cost — direct European Commission pushback, six weeks of public friction, and an eventual reversal that generated its own news cycle — arrived regardless of whichever reading of the booking data one prefers.

## The industry pushes back — and Brussels says no

By late June, the accumulated queue reports from Greece, Portugal, Spain, Italy, and Belgium had escalated into a coordinated industry response. IATA formally demanded a suspension of EES controls until the following summer, citing documented "delays and missed connections" across those five countries specifically. Ryanair's public posture hardened further: CEO Michael O'Leary warned travelers directly that flights would not wait for passengers stuck in EES queues, urging early arrival as the only reliable protection against a missed departure — a warning that followed the airline's own Athens–Luton incident.

On July 1, 2026, the pressure consolidated into a single, high-level appeal: ACI Europe, Airlines for Europe (A4E), and IATA — the three organizations representing essentially the entire European airport and airline industry — sent a joint letter to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. The letter characterized EES implementation as having reached a "critical point," warning of severe operational consequences and unsustainable pressure on border authorities, airports, and airlines alike. ACI Europe's language to Commission officials was blunt: the current implementation was "disrupting passengers and putting border authorities, airports and airlines under unsustainable pressure."

The European Commission's answer, delivered July 8 through Home Affairs Commissioner Magnus Brunner, was a clear no. Officials acknowledged roughly 20 "difficult spots" across the Schengen Area — an implicit admission that the rollout had not gone smoothly — but held that a full suspension was both "not needed" and "not possible." The stated reasoning returned to the same data-integrity logic that had doomed Greece's UK exemption: switching EES off in some countries while it continued running in others would risk exactly the kind of entry/exit mismatch that could wrongly flag legitimate travelers as overstayers, a worse outcome than queue delays. Brunner also pointed to causes outside EES itself — insufficient staffing and inadequate infrastructure at some border points — as contributing to the reported delays, distributing responsibility back toward national authorities rather than the system's design.

What the Commission did confirm, rather than deny, is that the flexibility mechanism already built into the rules remains available and in active use: individual border crossing points can pause biometric capture for up to six hours at a time when queues become excessive, under Regulation (EU) 2025/1534. This is the legal tool Greece was always entitled to use location-by-location — the one it had instead tried to apply nationality-wide in April and May. In its narrower, congestion-triggered form, the mechanism has been invoked repeatedly across the Schengen Area through the summer, including at Greek airports during peak arrival banks.

That flexibility, however, has a hard expiry: September 6, 2026, ninety days after the April 10 full rollout, with a possible 60-day extension available at the Commission's discretion. As of late July 2026, nine member states had formally requested that extension. The Commission had not yet granted it. For Greece — whose peak season extends well past the traditional July–August core into a growing September and October shoulder period that the site has covered separately — the closing of this safety valve just as autumn arrivals ramp up is one of the more consequential open questions for the remainder of the 2026 season.


## The legal mechanism, explained: what "flexibility" actually permits

Because so much of Greece's 2026 EES story turns on the difference between what is legally permitted and what Greece initially attempted, it is worth stating the mechanism precisely.

Regulation (EU) 2025/1534 allows a Schengen member state to partially pause biometric registration at a **specific, named border crossing point** for up to six hours at a time, renewable, when queues at that location become excessive — for a window running through the 90 days following the April 10, 2026 full rollout (i.e., through roughly early July), with a possible further 60-day extension pushing the mechanism's absolute limit to September 6, 2026. Each individual suspension must be formally notified to the European Commission, specifying which crossing point is affected and for how long. Crucially, the mechanism cannot be applied to a single nationality — it must apply to all non-EU travelers passing through that specific location during the pause, regardless of passport. And even when biometric capture is paused, the underlying EES database keeps running: airlines still transmit Advance Passenger Information, and any traveler's overstay status is still checked against whatever entry/exit records already exist for them.

This is the precise distinction that separates what Greece did in April (a standing, nationality-based policy) from what every Schengen country, including Greece, is legitimately doing throughout summer 2026 (short, location-specific, nationality-blind pauses during acute congestion). Both Portugal (at Lisbon, Porto, and Faro) and other member states have used the location-specific version of this tool extensively and without the same Commission pushback Greece received in April and May — because it complies with the letter of the regulation.


## Cruise and ferry passengers: EES at sea

EES applies at Greece's sea borders as well as its airports, though with an important nuance for cruise itineraries. The determining factor is not the port itself but whether the specific crossing involves an external Schengen border. A cruise or ferry moving between Turkey and Greece — for instance, the popular Turkey–Greek islands routes — crosses an external Schengen border and triggers full EES processing for non-EU passengers. A journey between Italy and Greece, both Schengen members, is treated as an internal Schengen movement and does not require a fresh EES entry. A multi-stop Mediterranean cruise that visits only Schengen ports does not typically generate a new EES registration at every single call; a cruise that also visits a non-Schengen country (again, Turkey being the most common Greek-itinerary example) does trigger additional exit and entry checks at that boundary.

Piraeus, Santorini, and Mykonos are among the Greek ports where EES infrastructure is active for non-EU passenger processing, alongside major cruise gateways across Spain, France, and Italy that have installed comparable seaport facilities through 2026. Unlike the full mandatory seaport deployment across the Schengen Area, which is not required until mid-2027, current Greek seaport EES processing runs alongside the broader summer 2026 pressure at air borders — meaning cruise lines and their passengers face many of the same queue dynamics documented at airports, concentrated around ship arrival and departure windows at Piraeus in particular. Industry warnings from ACI Europe and IATA specifically flagged potential four-to-six-hour queues at peak cruise-season crossings, and travel-industry reporting through the summer described active lobbying of Brussels to ease EES specifically around cruise-heavy port calls — a request that received the same "not needed, not possible" answer as the broader airline industry appeal.

For [travelers combining a Greek island itinerary with cruise arrivals](https://greektriplanner.me/blog/greece-ferry-guide), the practical implication is straightforward: non-EU passport holders should build in the same buffer time around ferry and cruise embarkation that they would around a flight connection, particularly on a first Schengen entry of the trip.


## What comes next: the September cliff and the ETIAS delay

Two forward-looking developments frame how the rest of 2026 is likely to play out for Greece and EES.

First, the flexibility mechanism's approaching expiry. If the Commission does not extend the six-hour pause tool beyond September 6, 2026, Greek border points lose one of their few legitimate tools for managing acute congestion just as the traditional peak season transitions into Greece's expanding shoulder season — a period the site has documented separately as an increasingly significant share of annual arrivals. Nine member states had requested an extension as of late July; whether that request succeeds will materially affect how smoothly October arrivals move through Athens and the islands.

Second, the parallel system that was originally meant to follow EES — ETIAS, the €20 pre-travel authorization required of visa-exempt non-EU travelers before they even depart for the Schengen Area — has itself been pushed back. ETIAS was, until mid-July 2026, still officially slated for the last quarter of 2026. Financial Times reporting in early July, since echoed across the travel press, revealed that eu-LISA's internal assessment now considers a 2026 launch "no longer feasible," with the delay attributed directly to the difficulties of the EES rollout consuming the same technical and administrative resources. The EU's official ETIAS website has since quietly removed its "last quarter of 2026" target, replacing it with a commitment to announce a specific date several months in advance. eu-LISA's management board was scheduled to revisit the timeline at a September 2026 meeting; industry estimates now point to April 2027 as the more realistic earliest launch, with the standard 6-month transitional enforcement period pushing full mandatory ETIAS compliance toward late 2027.

For practical purposes, this means non-EU travelers planning Greece trips for the remainder of 2026 — including the crucial autumn shoulder season — face no new advance-application requirement beyond the at-border EES registration already in effect. The additional friction of applying for, and paying for, pre-travel authorization before booking a Greece trip remains, for now, a 2027 problem rather than a 2026 one.

## What non-EU travelers to Greece need to know right now

For a traveler planning a Greece trip for the remainder of 2026, the practical situation is more manageable than the headlines suggest, provided a few basics are in place.

EES applies to anyone holding a passport from outside the EU/EEA — Americans, Britons, Canadians, Australians, and most other nationalities — making a short-term visit of up to 90 days in any rolling 180-day period. No advance registration or application is required or possible; the process happens entirely at the physical border, whether that is an airport, a seaport, or a land crossing. On a first Schengen entry anywhere in 2026, expect fingerprint and facial-image capture; on any subsequent entry within three years, expect a quicker face-match rather than full re-registration. The process itself, per the European Commission, takes roughly 70 seconds once a traveler reaches the booth or kiosk — the queue beforehand is where the real time cost sits.

The single most useful piece of practical advice repeated across airline, airport, and travel-industry guidance is to build in more buffer time than pre-EES habits suggest: at least 2.5–3 hours before a departing flight at Athens specifically, and correspondingly more caution around tight connections anywhere in the Schengen Area, since a missed connection caused by a border queue is generally treated as outside the airline's control for compensation purposes. The same logic applies to island-hopping itineraries that pair a flight into Athens with a same-day domestic connection or ferry departure — a delay at initial passport control can cascade through an entire day's onward travel plan. [Travelers should also review current entry requirements](https://greektriplanner.me/blog/greece-travel-requirements-for-us-citizens) before departure, since documentation standards (passport validity windows, in particular) remain unchanged by EES itself even as the border process around them has changed.

Re-entry on a later 2026 trip, or a future visit within the three-year validity window, should be materially faster than a first crossing — the biggest queues by far are concentrated among first-time registrants, which is most of the traveling public in EES's first full year of operation.

A cruise or ferry moving between two Schengen countries (such as Italy and Greece) is treated as an internal Schengen movement and does not trigger a new EES entry. Piraeus, Santorini, and Mykonos are among the Greek ports with active EES processing infrastructure for non-EU cruise and ferry arrivals in 2026.
- Q: Do I need ETIAS to visit Greece in 2026? A: No. ETIAS, the separate €20 pre-travel authorization system for visa-exempt non-EU travelers, has been delayed from its original last-quarter-2026 target into 2027, following resourcing conflicts with the EES rollout. As of the most recent official guidance, no ETIAS application is required or even possible yet — travelers to Greece for the remainder of 2026 need only complete the at-border EES registration already in effect, not any advance online authorization.
- Q: What is EES and who does it affect? A: The Entry/Exit System (EES) is the EU's digital border-control system, fully operational since April 10, 2026 across all 29 Schengen countries. It replaces manual passport stamping with biometric registration — four fingerprints and a facial photograph — for non-EU, non-EEA nationals making short-term visits (up to 90 days in any 180-day period). It affects most American, British, Canadian, and Australian travelers, among others. The registration is free, happens entirely at the border with no advance application, and remains valid for three years per traveler. EU and EEA citizens, along with Ireland and Cyprus (which sit outside Schengen), are not affected.
- Q: How can I avoid long EES queues when flying into Greece? A: Build in significantly more buffer time than pre-2026 habits suggest — at least 2.5 to 3 hours before a departing flight at Athens specifically, and extra caution around any tight same-day connection or ferry departure following an initial Schengen entry. First-time registrants face the longest waits; a second or subsequent 2026 crossing, or any crossing within three years of first registration, should be materially faster since it requires only a quick face-match rather than full biometric capture. Missed connections caused by border queues are generally treated as outside airline control for compensation purposes, so the buffer is the traveler's own responsibility to build in.
- Q: Why did Greece try to exempt UK travelers from EES in the first place? A: British travelers are Greece's single largest non-EU visitor market — outnumbering every other non-European nationality combined in 2025, with 4.89 million arrivals and €3.74 billion in spending. Some island airports handle more than 2,000 UK arrivals and departures on peak days. Greek officials, led by Tourism Minister Olga Kefalogianni, argued that applying full biometric registration to that volume risked serious congestion and framed the exemption as protecting the visitor experience for Greece's most important tourism market, even as the European Commission maintained the move was not legally permitted as a standing, nationality-wide policy.
