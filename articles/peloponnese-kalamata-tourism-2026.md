# Kalamata Airport Just Got a €125 Million, 40-Year Bet. The Peloponnese Is Still Greece's Smallest Tourism Region.

**Subtitle:** In June 2026, Greece signed a 40-year concession handing Kalamata's small regional airport to a Fraport-led consortium, with a stated ambition to nearly double its passenger traffic by 2030. The catch: the Peloponnese remains one of the smallest tourism regions in the country by revenue, even as it posts some of Greece's fastest percentage growth. Here's the full data picture — the concession, the numbers, and the luxury resort quietly carrying much of the region's economy.

**Excerpt:** On 22 June 2026, Greece signed a 40-year, roughly €125 million concession for Kalamata International Airport, awarded to a consortium led by Fraport AG alongside the Copelouzos and Constantakopoulos groups — the same family behind Costa Navarino, the luxury resort that has arguably done more to put this corner of the Peloponnese on the map than any airport statistic. Kalamata carried a record 368,515 passengers in 2025; Fraport Greece wants to push that toward 700,000 by 2030. Meanwhile, the wider Peloponnese region remains one of Greece's smallest tourism economies by revenue — sharing, with seven other regions, less than a tenth of the country's tourism receipts — even as it posts some of the fastest percentage growth of anywhere in the country. This report lays out the concession terms, the traffic and route data, the Costa Navarino economics, and why a region with excellent road access to Athens is now also betting on a rebuilt airport.

## Key Takeaways (for Sanity keyTakeaways field)

1. Greece signed a 40-year concession for Kalamata International Airport on June 22, 2026, awarded to Kalamata Airport S.A. — owned by Fraport AG, Delta Airport Investments (Copelouzos Group), and Pileas Holdings (Constantakopoulos Group), with Greece's National Development Fund (Growthfund) taking a 10% stake. The deal totals roughly €125 million in investment against a €45 million upfront payment, funding a new terminal, expanded check-in and security areas, and modernized technology — the airport's most significant upgrade since its 1991 terminal.

2. Kalamata carried a record 368,515 passengers in 2025, up 8.7% on 2024 and continuing to grow 12.7% in early 2026, with international travelers now making up 92.4% of traffic. Fraport Greece has stated an explicit ambition to nearly double that figure to around 700,000 passengers annually by 2030 — a target that depends heavily on the new route network taking hold.

3. The 2026 season brings Kalamata to 22 destinations via 31 direct routes, concentrated in the UK, Germany, and Central Europe — its three strongest source markets. New for 2026: Jet2 from Edinburgh and London Gatwick, Condor from Hamburg, Wizz Air from Budapest, and British Airways' new Gatwick–Kalamata route launched April 30, 2026.

4. Despite this momentum, the Peloponnese remains genuinely small in Greece's tourism economy: it is one of eight regions the Bank of Greece classifies outside the "top five" tourism earners, together accounting for just €2.115 billion of Greece's total 2024 tourism receipts, while the five leading regions — led by the South Aegean's €5.687 billion — captured 89.7% of the total. At the same time, the Peloponnese posted some of the fastest percentage growth of any Greek region in 2025, including a reported 43% jump in travel receipts across major foreign markets and a 141% increase in receipts among the country's six largest source markets — the largest such increase nationally, though both figures come off a very small base.

5. Costa Navarino, the roughly 1,000-hectare luxury resort complex in Messinia developed by the Constantakopoulos family's TEMES (now under holding company ĒNSOFI), is the clearest single driver of the region's premium tourism demand — contributing an estimated 11% of Messinia's regional GDP between 2006 and 2022, employing over 2,500 people in peak season, and posting 2024 revenue of €133.4 million, its strongest year on record. Its four hotels, four golf courses, and expanding wellness and residential offerings are the demand engine that makes the Kalamata airport bet plausible.

6. The Peloponnese holds a genuine structural advantage over Greece's island destinations: the A7 "Moreas" motorway cuts Athens–Kalamata driving time to roughly two hours, meaning the region can capture both domestic road-trip demand and international fly-in visitors — a hybrid accessibility model unlike any major Greek island, and one the airport concession is explicitly designed to extend rather than replace.

## ARTICLE BODY

There is a particular kind of Greek tourism story that doesn't fit neatly into "record year" headlines, and Kalamata and the wider Peloponnese are living it right now. On one hand: a genuinely significant infrastructure deal — a 40-year airport concession, a European aviation group's name attached to it, an explicit target to nearly double passenger traffic within four years. On the other hand: a region that, by the numbers, remains one of the smallest players in Greek tourism, still dwarfed by Crete, the South Aegean, and Attica by an order of magnitude.

Both things are true simultaneously, and understanding why requires looking past the headline concession figure into what's actually driving Peloponnese tourism today — road access from Athens, a single extraordinarily influential luxury resort complex, and a bet that better air connectivity can turn a fast-growing-but-small market into something genuinely significant. This report walks through all of it: the concession terms, the traffic data, the route network, the regional economics, and the Costa Navarino story that sits at the center of nearly everything else.

## The concession: what Greece actually signed

On **June 22, 2026**, at the Growthfund Investor Summit, Greece formally signed a **40-year concession agreement** for Kalamata International Airport "Captain Vassilis Constantakopoulos" (IATA: KLX) — a deal between the Hellenic Republic, Greece's National Development Fund (Growthfund) as grantor, and the newly formed concessionaire **Kalamata Airport S.A.**

The ownership structure ties together three names that matter for understanding the deal's logic: **Fraport AG**, the German airport-operations group that already runs 14 other regional Greek airports; **Delta Airport Investments**, part of the Copelouzos Group; and **Pileas Holdings**, part of the **Constantakopoulos Group** — the same family behind Costa Navarino, discussed in detail below. Initial equity was set at Fraport 51%, Delta 24.5%, and Pileas 24.5%, before Growthfund exercised a right to acquire a **10% stake "without consideration,"** proportionally diluting the other shareholders to roughly Fraport 45.9%, Delta 22.05%, and Pileas 22.05%.

Financially, the winning bid carried an upfront consideration of **€45 million** (€45.2 million in constant 2025 prices), against a **total investment programme of approximately €125 million** — Greek Minister of State Kyriakos Pierrakakis cited a €120 million figure in public remarks, a difference that reflects rounding rather than a discrepancy in the underlying deal. Of that total, **€28.3 million is committed within the first three years**, and Growthfund's expected total return across fees and dividends over the concession's life is estimated at €71.2 million.

What the investment actually buys: a new terminal building; check-in counters expanded **from 4 to 10**; substantially enlarged security-screening and passport-control areas; upgraded aircraft parking capacity; new retail, food and beverage, and commercial space; and new operational technology across roughly 11,200 sq.m. of new or reconstructed facilities. Officials describe it as the airport's most significant physical transformation since its original 1991 terminal building.

**How this compares to Fraport's bigger Greek deal.** Fraport Greece is not new to Greek regional airports — in April 2017, the same group (then majority-owned by Fraport AG alongside Copelouzos) began operating **14 regional Greek airports** under a separate 40-year concession, paying an upfront fee of **€1.234 billion**, the largest concession fee in Greek aviation history, plus ongoing fixed and EBITDA-linked variable payments. That 14-airport network is forecast to carry a record **~37 million passengers in 2025** and recently launched a further €200 million investment cycle on top of more than €2 billion already invested. Set next to that, the Kalamata deal — roughly €125 million in total investment for a single airport carrying under 400,000 passengers — is a fraction of the scale, but it signals the same operator applying its regional-airport playbook to a genuinely underdeveloped gateway rather than an established one. [For the fuller picture of how Greece's airports collectively handle international arrivals](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-airports-passenger-traffic), Kalamata sits firmly among the country's smallest, which is precisely why the growth target attached to it is so ambitious in relative terms.


## The traffic numbers: from 302,969 to a 700,000 target

Kalamata's passenger growth has been steady, if modest in absolute terms, over the past three years:

| Year | Passengers | YoY Change | International Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 | 302,969 | — | — |
| 2024 | 338,892 | +11.9% | — |
| 2025 | 368,515 | +8.7% | 92.4% |
| 2026 (Jan–Apr) | — | +12.7% | — |

*Source: Kalamata International Airport official statistics; Fraport Greece.*

The international share is worth pausing on: at 92.4% of 2025 traffic, Kalamata is overwhelmingly a foreign-visitor gateway rather than a domestic-connection airport, which tracks with its position as a leisure destination for UK, German, and Central European travelers rather than a hub serving Greek domestic routes.

Against that base, Fraport Greece's stated target — via George Vilos, the company's General Director of Development, speaking in November 2025 — is explicit: **"the airport will be able to double its traffic by 2030, reaching nearly 700,000 passengers annually — from around 380,000 today."** That is close to a 90% increase over roughly four to five years, a materially faster growth rate than the 14-airport Fraport network as a whole has managed even in its strongest recent seasons, and it depends almost entirely on the new route network actually converting into sustained demand — which is where the 2026 season's specific additions matter most.


## The 2026 route map: 22 destinations, 31 routes

The Region of Peloponnese announced the 2026 network in February 2026: **22 destinations reachable via 31 direct routes**, concentrated overwhelmingly in the UK, Germany, and Central Europe — Kalamata's three strongest demand markets.

| Carrier | New/Notable 2026 Route | Launch |
|---|---|---|
| Jet2 | Edinburgh–Kalamata | New for 2026 |
| Jet2 | London Gatwick–Kalamata | New for 2026 |
| British Airways | London Gatwick–Kalamata | Launched April 30, 2026 |
| Condor | Hamburg–Kalamata | New for 2026 |
| Wizz Air | Budapest–Kalamata | New for 2026 |
| Discover Airlines | Munich–Kalamata | Extended season, into November |
| Eurowings | Düsseldorf–Kalamata | Extended season, into November |

*Additional established carriers serving Kalamata in 2025/26 include Aegean Airlines (the airport's largest operator by scheduled departures), easyJet, Ryanair, Edelweiss, Austrian Airlines, Transavia and Transavia France, and Arkia.*

Two things stand out in this network. First, it deliberately deepens Kalamata's two strongest existing markets — the UK and Germany — rather than chasing entirely new geographies, with the exception of Budapest, which opens a genuinely new Central European corridor via Wizz Air. Second, the frequency and season-length increases (Munich and Düsseldorf both extending into November) point toward an explicit shoulder-season strategy, extending Kalamata's usable season beyond the traditional June–September core — a strategy that mirrors the golf and wellness positioning discussed later in this report.

Behind the route additions sits real public money: the Region of Peloponnese nearly doubled its airline co-marketing budget for the 2024–2026 period, with total co-advertising investment expected to reach approximately **€2 million by 2026**, according to Regional Governor Dimitris Ptochos — a direct subsidy aimed at de-risking new route launches for the airlines adding them.


## The Peloponnese in Greece's tourism economy: fast growth, small base

Here is where the Kalamata airport story needs to be read against a wider, more sobering regional picture. The Bank of Greece's regional tourism-receipts data for 2024 divides Greece's 13 regions into a clear hierarchy, and the Peloponnese sits well down that list:

| Region | 2024 Tourism Receipts |
|---|---|
| South Aegean | €5,687.4 million |
| Attica | €4,750.9 million |
| Crete | €4,568.7 million |
| Ionian Islands | €1,983.6 million |
| Central Macedonia | €1,486.0 million |
| **Peloponnese + 7 other regions (combined)** | **€2,115.1 million** |

*Source: Bank of Greece, 2024 final regional tourism figures.*

The top five regions alone captured **89.7%** of Greece's total tourism receipts in 2024, leaving the Peloponnese to share the remaining €2.1 billion with seven other regions — meaning the Peloponnese's individual share is smaller still than that combined figure suggests. Within the region, the average spend per overnight stay in 2024 was **€69** (essentially flat year-on-year), with an average length of stay of 7.1 nights, itself down 6% from the prior year.

And yet, set against that small base, 2025 growth rates were genuinely striking. Bank of Greece data cited by the Region of Peloponnese showed, across major foreign markets specifically: overnight stays rising from 1.87 million to **2.27 million (+21.3%)**; visitor arrivals from 271,800 to **347,100 (+27.8%)**; and travel receipts from €110.9 million to **€158.6 million (+43%)**. Separately, INSETE's "Who Goes Where?" study of Greece's six largest source markets found the Peloponnese posted a **141% increase in receipts** — the single largest regional increase in the country, ahead of Attica's 42% — which INSETE explicitly framed as evidence of "the potential for geographical expansion beyond the traditional island destinations."

The honest read on these two data points together: **the percentage growth is real, but it is growth on a genuinely small base**, and the region's air-arrival numbers make that concrete. Peloponnese international air arrivals for January–June 2026 totaled just **83,000 (+3.1%)** — compare that to Crete's 2.1 million (+8.8%) over the same period, or the Dodecanese's 1.6 million, or the Ionian Islands' 1.4 million. [Set against Greece's overall 2025 tourism performance](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-tourism-statistics-2025) — a near-38 million-visitor, €22.4 billion national market — the Peloponnese remains, in absolute terms, a rounding error next to the country's major island destinations, however fast its percentage growth runs.

Local industry voices have been candid about the constraint the airport concession is meant to address. Messinia Hoteliers Association president Dimitris Karalis noted in mid-2025 that foreign arrivals "remain limited due to poor airport infrastructure," while also pointing to the region's genuine advantage: it "always [has] the advantage of easy road access" — the theme this report returns to below.


## Costa Navarino: the demand engine behind the airport bet

It is difficult to discuss Peloponnese tourism economics without arriving quickly at **Costa Navarino**, the roughly 1,000-hectare luxury resort development in Messinia, and doing so is genuinely necessary to understand why Kalamata's airport concession makes commercial sense at all.

Developed by **TEMES S.A.** — the Constantakopoulos family company, restructured in 2025 under a new parent holding, **ĒNSOFI S.A.** — Costa Navarino comprises four 5-star hotels (The Westin Resort, The Romanos, W Costa Navarino, and Mandarin Oriental, roughly 1,100 keys combined), four signature 18-hole golf courses, more than 40 dining venues, extensive spa facilities, and the Mouratoglou Tennis Center, with the resort's built footprint occupying under 10% of its total land area.

The investment scale is substantial: direct investment exceeded **€850 million by 2023**, with total investment including third-party development surpassing **€1.25 billion**, and projections for the completed vision exceeding **€1.2 billion direct and €2.5 billion including third-party investment**. Ownership sits roughly 75% with the Constantakopoulos family and 25% with Saudi Arabia's Olayan Group.

The regional economic footprint is the number that matters most for this report's argument: TEMES has been credited with contributing an estimated **11% of Messinia's regional GDP between 2006 and 2022**. Direct employment exceeds 2,500 in peak season (with roughly 55% of 2024 hires drawn from the local community), and at full build-out the group projects more than **11,000 combined direct and indirect jobs**. TEMES's own 2024 audited results — published November 2025 — showed revenue of **€133.4 million**, up from €112.9 million in 2023, and gross profit of €36.1 million, described internally as potentially the group's strongest year to date.

[This kind of concentrated, single-developer economic impact is unusual even by the standards of Greece's broader luxury hotel investment wave](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-luxury-tourism-economics), where investment tends to be spread across many independent operators rather than anchored by one vertically integrated resort complex. Costa Navarino's expansion pipeline extends the pattern further: **Navarino Hills** is being developed into a year-round wellness and sports resort; **Navarino Blue**, near Rizomylos — a ten-minute drive from Kalamata airport — is planned as a multi-resort beachfront development; and a new winery is expanding bottle capacity sixfold, from 50,000 to over 300,000 bottles annually. Group-adjacent projects extend Costa Navarino's model to Athens (The Ilisian, a roughly €340 million Conrad/Waldorf Astoria project with partial 2025 operations) and to a 40-year Pylos Marina concession secured in 2024.

The causal logic connecting all of this to the airport concession is straightforward, if worth stating plainly: Costa Navarino created a base of premium international demand — and a base of second-home buyers — large enough to make direct UK and German flight routes commercially viable in the first place. The airport concession is, in significant part, a bet that this demand base will keep growing fast enough to fill an expanded terminal.


## Source markets: UK, Germany, and an unexploited US opportunity

Kalamata's route network reflects its actual demand base closely: the **United Kingdom** is the dominant market, followed by **Germany**, with the **Netherlands** and a scattering of Scandinavian, Swiss, French, and Austrian routes filling out the network.

National-level INSETE data (via GTP Headlines, July 2026) adds useful context. The **UK posted the highest tourism-receipt growth of Greece's six biggest source markets in 2025, at +18% year-on-year**, with the highest average spend per visit among those six markets, at **€729** — and INSETE's study specifically flagged "stronger British interest in the Peloponnese" as part of a broader diversification story beyond the traditional islands. [Britain's outsized and still-growing role across Greek tourism generally](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-tourism-source-markets) is especially pronounced in the Peloponnese's route map, where UK carriers (Jet2, British Airways, easyJet) now anchor the airport's growth strategy.

The more interesting gap is the US market. INSETE data shows American visitors are the highest per-night spenders in Greece overall, at **€121 per night**, and the second-highest per-trip spenders at **€715** — but that spending is, per INSETE, "heavily concentrated in Attica and the South Aegean," representing what the organization itself calls "a significant development opportunity" for regions like the Peloponnese that currently see little of it. No direct US routes currently serve Kalamata, and closing that gap — likely via connections through Athens rather than direct transatlantic service, given the airport's scale — represents one of the clearer paths to lifting the region's still-modest average spend figures.


## Golf, wellness, and the shoulder-season strategy

Costa Navarino's four Troon-managed golf courses make the Peloponnese Greece's clear leader in golf tourism, a niche the resort actively cultivates through events like the **Aegean Messinia Pro-Am**, which marked its 20th anniversary edition in June 2026. Golf tourism carries structural advantages for exactly the problem this report keeps returning to: it is high-spend, skews toward exactly the demographic that can absorb premium pricing, and — critically — extends naturally into shoulder-season months when island beach tourism drops off sharply, directly supporting the extended-season route strategy (Munich and Düsseldorf into November) described above.

The Peloponnese has also emerged as a leading destination within Greece's wedding-tourism sector, led again largely by Costa Navarino and the Mani peninsula, per INSETE data reported in Tovima — another high-value, low-volume niche that fits the region's broader positioning strategy. [This mirrors a wider national push into MICE and special-interest tourism](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-mice-business-tourism) as a tool for reducing Greece's structural over-reliance on peak-summer beach demand, with the Peloponnese arguably further along that path than most regions, purely because Costa Navarino's business model has always depended on it.


## The accessibility advantage no Greek island has

Here is the structural point that separates the Peloponnese from every comparison this report could otherwise make to Greece's island destinations: **it doesn't actually need an airport to be accessible from Athens.**

The **A7 "Moreas" Motorway** — running from Corinth through Tripoli to Kalamata, roughly 205km, with a branch to Sparti — cut Athens–Kalamata driving time to approximately **two hours**, roughly halving the previous journey. Held under a 32-year concession by Moreas S.A. (part of the Aktor Group) since 2007 and funded through tolls, the motorway carried an average of roughly 41,266 vehicles daily as of 2013 data, and its concession terms explicitly bar the Greek state from building a competing parallel motorway. A separately completed Patras–Pyrgos highway improves western Peloponnese access, and a highway connecting Kalamata to Rizomylos, Pylos, and Methoni — directly serving the Costa Navarino coastline — remains under construction.

[This dual-access model — a fast, tolled motorway from the capital plus a growing international airport — is genuinely distinctive among the destinations this site covers](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/halkidiki-tourism-statistics). Halkidiki, in northern Greece, built its tourism economy almost entirely on road access, with roughly half its arrivals crossing land borders from Romania, Bulgaria, and Serbia rather than flying at all. Greece's island destinations sit at the opposite extreme: air or sea arrival is the *only* option, full stop, meaning any airport disruption — an air-traffic-control problem, an EES border-queue crisis, a strike — has no alternative outlet. The Peloponnese occupies a genuinely different position: **capable of absorbing domestic Athens weekend traffic by road even in a scenario where its airport underperforms**, while simultaneously building international air capacity to capture visitors who would never consider a destination reachable only by a multi-hour drive. That resilience is arguably a more durable competitive advantage than the airport concession's headline passenger targets — though the concession clearly aims to make the air-arrival side of that equation far more significant than it currently is.


## What's adding momentum in 2026

Beyond the airport story, several developments are adding genuine cultural and commercial momentum to the region through 2026. **Ancient Messene**, Messinia's flagship archaeological site — one of the best-preserved ancient Greek cities, excavated over decades by the late Petros Themelis (d. 2023) and a 2011 Europa Nostra Award winner — has visitor-friendly entrance upgrades under review by Greece's Central Archaeological Council, alongside a proposed new archaeological museum under joint study by the Ministry of Culture and the Region of Peloponnese, and a planned fire-protection network for the site.

Perhaps more significant for international awareness: Messinia gained substantial global cultural exposure through Christopher Nolan's *The Odyssey*, partly filmed in the region and released July 17, 2026 — timing that dovetails directly with Costa Navarino's own Odyssey-themed 2026 programming. Condé Nast Traveler separately named the area a "Best Place to Go in 2026," and The Times included Kalamata among its best European city breaks for the year — the kind of editorial attention that tends to precede, rather than follow, genuine demand growth in destinations still building out their infrastructure.

Nationally, Greek tourism revenue rose a striking **36.9% to €2.79 billion in January–April 2026**, with arrivals up 27.1% to 5.24 million — though this figure reflects some calendar effects (Easter timing, a 67.8% surge specifically in land-border arrivals) rather than a uniform national acceleration, and should be read as broader context rather than Peloponnese-specific momentum.

A structural limitation applies throughout this report and should be read carefully: Peloponnese-specific growth figures cited from different sources (the Region of Peloponnese's Bank of Greece-sourced +21.3%/+27.8%/+43% figures; INSETE's separate +141% six-market receipts figure) reflect **different market subsets and different comparison bases**, not a single consistent measurement of "total Peloponnese tourism growth." Both are presented with their specific scope clearly attributed, and neither should be read as describing the region's overall tourism performance in isolation from the region's still-small absolute base, detailed in the regional receipts table above.

**Primary sources:** as listed above.

The commonly cited Costa Navarino room-count figures carry minor variance across sources (for example, the W Costa Navarino is listed as 226 rooms on the property's own site versus 246 in some third-party sources); this report uses the widely cited approximate total of 1,100 keys across all four hotels. Several forward-looking figures in this report — the 700,000-passenger 2030 target, Navarino Blue's development timeline, new-terminal delivery dates, and TEMES's longer-term job and economic-footprint projections — describe announced plans and stated targets rather than completed facts, and are flagged as such throughout.

A: On June 22, 2026, Greece signed a 40-year concession for Kalamata International Airport with Kalamata Airport S.A., a consortium owned by Fraport AG (45.9%), Delta Airport Investments/Copelouzos Group (22.05%), Pileas Holdings/Constantakopoulos Group (22.05%), and Greece's National Development Fund (Growthfund, 10%). The deal totals approximately €125 million in investment, including a €45 million upfront payment, funding a new terminal, expanded check-in and security facilities, and modernized technology.
- Q: How many passengers does Kalamata Airport currently handle? A: Kalamata carried a record 368,515 passengers in 2025, up 8.7% from 2024's 338,892, with international travelers making up 92.4% of that total. Traffic continued growing 12.7% in the first four months of 2026. Fraport Greece has stated a target of nearly doubling annual traffic to around 700,000 passengers by 2030.
- Q: What new flight routes does Kalamata have in 2026? A: The 2026 season brings Kalamata to 22 destinations via 31 direct routes. New additions include Jet2 from Edinburgh and London Gatwick, Condor from Hamburg, Wizz Air from Budapest, and British Airways' new Gatwick–Kalamata route, launched April 30, 2026. Existing carriers include Aegean Airlines (the largest operator), easyJet, Ryanair, and several German/Austrian/Swiss carriers.
- Q: How big is Peloponnese tourism compared to the rest of Greece? A: Genuinely small in absolute terms. Bank of Greece data show the Peloponnese sharing, with seven other regions, just €2.115 billion of Greece's total 2024 tourism receipts, while the top five regions alone (led by the South Aegean at €5.687 billion) captured 89.7% of the national total. However, the Peloponnese posted some of the fastest percentage growth of any Greek region in 2025, including a reported 43% rise in travel receipts across major foreign markets.
- Q: What is Costa Navarino and why does it matter for Peloponnese tourism? A: Costa Navarino is a roughly 1,000-hectare luxury resort complex in Messinia, developed by the Constantakopoulos family's TEMES, comprising four 5-star hotels, four golf courses, and extensive spa and dining facilities. It is estimated to have contributed 11% of Messinia's regional GDP between 2006 and 2022, employs over 2,500 people in peak season, and posted 2024 revenue of €133.4 million. It is widely credited as the primary driver of the premium international demand that makes Kalamata's airport expansion commercially viable.
- Q: Can you visit the Peloponnese without flying into Kalamata? A: Yes — this is one of the region's structural advantages over Greek islands. The A7 "Moreas" Motorway connects Athens to Kalamata in roughly two hours by car, meaning the Peloponnese can be reached easily by road from the capital, unlike island destinations that require air or ferry travel. This dual accessibility (road plus a growing international airport) is uncommon among Greece's major tourism destinations.
- Q: Which countries send the most tourists to Kalamata and the Peloponnese? A: The United Kingdom is the dominant source market, followed by Germany, with the Netherlands, Scandinavia, Switzerland, France, and Austria also represented in the route network. The UK posted the highest tourism-receipt growth of Greece's six biggest source markets in 2025 (+18%), and INSETE specifically noted growing British interest in the Peloponnese. The US market, Greece's highest per-night spender nationally, remains underrepresented in the region and is flagged by INSETE as a significant growth opportunity.
