## ARTICLE BODY

There is a particular kind of irony in how ETIAS ended up delayed, and it is worth stating plainly at the outset: the system that was supposed to make entering Europe smoother got pushed back because the system that came before it made entering Europe harder.

ETIAS — the European Travel Information and Authorisation System — is the EU's version of what the United States calls ESTA or the UK calls its ETA: a simple, low-cost, online pre-travel screening step for visa-exempt visitors, completed before departure rather than at the border. It was designed to work in tandem with the Entry/Exit System (EES), the biometric border-registration system that finally went fully live across Greece and the rest of the Schengen Area on April 10, 2026. The plan, as officially stated for years, was for ETIAS to follow EES by roughly six months, landing in the last quarter of 2026.

That plan collapsed over the course of summer 2026 — not because ETIAS itself had some new technical failure, but because EES had one first, at scale, and the resulting mess absorbed exactly the resources, attention, and political appetite that ETIAS needed to launch on schedule. This report covers what has actually been confirmed, what remains genuinely uncertain, what the delay means in practice for anyone planning a Greece trip through 2026 and into 2027, and how the system will actually work once it does arrive.

## EES vs. ETIAS: two different systems, one shared delay

Confusion between these two systems is extremely common, and understandable — both are new, both are EU-wide, both target non-EU visitors, and both have been in the news for the same reason (border friction) at the same time. They are, however, structurally different tools solving different problems, and Greece-bound travelers benefit from understanding exactly which one currently applies to them.

| | **EES (Entry/Exit System)** | **ETIAS (Travel Information and Authorisation System)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Status as of August 2026** | Fully operational since April 10, 2026 | Not yet launched; delayed to 2027 |
| **What it does** | Records entry/exit electronically at the physical border; replaces passport stamps | Pre-screens visa-exempt travelers *before* departure, online |
| **When you interact with it** | At the airport, seaport, or land border, in person | Before you even book or leave home, via a website or app |
| **Biometrics required** | Yes — fingerprints and facial scan on first crossing | No — it is a screening questionnaire, not a biometric system |
| **Cost** | Free | €20 (waived under 18 / over 70) |
| **Who it applies to** | All non-EU/EEA short-stay visitors | Visa-exempt non-EU/EEA nationals specifically (roughly 59 countries) |
| **Validity once obtained** | 3 years per traveler | 3 years or until passport expiry, whichever is sooner |
| **Do you need it for Greece right now?** | **Yes** — already in effect | **No** — not yet operational, no action possible or required |

The critical practical point for anyone reading this ahead of a Greece trip: **EES is real, active, and already affects you if you hold a non-EU passport. ETIAS is not yet real in any operational sense** — there is no application to file, no fee to pay, and no way to "get ahead of it," regardless of what third-party websites claiming to process early applications might suggest.


## The delay, reconstructed: what actually happened in 2026

The official position, for most of 2026, never wavered on paper. The EU's ETIAS portal maintained a "last quarter of 2026" target throughout the spring and into early summer, and both the European Commission and eu-LISA — the agency formally responsible for building and operating large-scale EU IT systems including ETIAS — continued to describe preparations as ongoing without signaling any change.

Behind that steady public messaging, something different was happening. In early July 2026, the Financial Times reported that eu-LISA had internally concluded that meeting the end-of-2026 target was "no longer feasible." Three people described as briefed on the internal discussions gave the FT consistent accounts; one, describing the prospect of an on-schedule 2026 launch, used a single blunt word: "illusory." Not every official inside the process shared that pessimism — one EU source suggested the eventual delay might prove brief, needing "only another quarter or another month" beyond the original target — but the direction of travel was unmistakable.

The FT's sourcing pointed to two intertwined causes, one technical and one strategic. On the technical side, one person briefed on the discussions described "still some IT issues" within ETIAS's own development. On the strategic side — and this is the more consequential explanation for understanding *why* the timing landed where it did — EU Home Affairs Commissioner Magnus Brunner acknowledged that part of the delay traced back to member states themselves: insufficient staffing, inadequate border infrastructure, and problems connected directly to the ongoing EES rollout. In other words, launching a second major new border-technology requirement on top of a first one that was actively generating three-to-six-hour airport queues — IATA's own words for what European airports experienced with EES that summer — risked compounding a crisis rather than resolving one.

The eu-LISA management board met on June 17, 2026 specifically to discuss ETIAS's "entry into operation." A spokesperson confirmed the meeting took place but, notably, offered no further detail afterward, telling reporters simply that there had "been no further developments on this topic" since. The board's next scheduled discussion falls in September 2026, and it is that meeting — not any single news report — that will most likely determine when, or whether, the Commission formally announces a revised launch date.

By mid-July, the shift became visible in the one place that matters most for travelers: the official ETIAS website itself quietly dropped its "last quarter of 2026" language. In its place, the site now states only that the European Union "will inform about the specific date for the start of ETIAS several months prior to its launch" — a formulation that commits to nothing beyond advance notice, whenever the actual decision is made.

## A brief history of a very delayed system

ETIAS's 2026-to-2027 slip is not an aberration — it is the continuation of a pattern that predates this year by half a decade. The system was first conceived under a 2018 EU regulation and originally targeted for launch in 2021.

| Announced Target | Outcome |
|---|---|
| 2021 | Delayed |
| 2022 | Delayed |
| 2023 | Delayed |
| 2024 | Delayed |
| 2025 | Delayed |
| Last quarter of 2026 | Delayed (confirmed July 2026) |
| **2027 (likely ~April)** | Current best estimate — not yet officially confirmed |

Most of the earlier delays trace back to the same root cause repeating itself: ETIAS's design makes it fundamentally dependent on the Entry/Exit System's underlying database, since ETIAS decisions rely on cross-checking applicant data against EES and other EU security systems. EES itself took years longer than originally planned to reach full deployment, finally achieving that status on April 10, 2026 — and even that achievement immediately generated the operational strain that has now pushed ETIAS back yet again. The two systems have effectively been stuck in a sequential dependency loop for the better part of six years.


## What this means for Greece specifically

Greece enters this delay from a position where the stakes are genuinely high. Tourism drives roughly 30% of Greek GDP directly and indirectly, and non-EU visitors — precisely the population ETIAS is designed to screen — represented the fastest-growing share of [Greece's 37.98 million 2025 international arrivals](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-tourism-statistics-2025), up 10% year-on-year against 2.8% EU growth. The UK alone sent [4.89 million visitors in 2025](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-tourism-source-markets), and American arrivals, while smaller in volume, carry the highest per-trip spend of any major market. Every one of those travelers would eventually need ETIAS; none of them need it yet.

That "not yet" is doing real work for Greece's tourism sector this year. Travel businesses across France, Spain, Italy, Germany, Greece, and Portugal — the EU's largest inbound-tourism economies — are all affected identically by the delay, since ETIAS was always designed as a single EU-wide system rather than a country-by-country rollout. For destinations like Greece that are simultaneously managing [the operational strain of EES itself](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-ees-biometric-borders-2026) — Athens Airport queues running 2.5–3 hours at peak in July 2026, an active industry campaign to extend the EU's biometric-pause flexibility past its September 6 deadline — the postponement of a second, unrelated new requirement is close to unambiguous good news. It removes one more variable from an already complicated summer, and pushes the question of "how do we introduce advance travel authorization without making queues worse" into a period when EES itself should, at least in theory, be running more smoothly.

The UK's business travel sector was explicit about this logic. Industry commentary welcomed the ETIAS delay specifically because introducing a second major system before airports had "sufficient time to adapt to EES" carried a real risk of compounding existing congestion rather than easing it — echoing IATA regional vice president Rafael Schvartzman's early-June warning that EES alone was already producing "unacceptable" waits of three to six hours at some European airports. For Greek tourism operators, tour companies, and the [affiliate and booking ecosystem built around Greek travel](https://greektriplanner.me/blog/greece-travel-requirements-for-us-citizens), the message is the same: one border-technology fire at a time is preferable to two simultaneously.

There is a modest complication worth naming directly. Some earlier 2026 coverage of Greek tourism — including reporting published before this summer's EES rollout fully played out — described ETIAS as being on track for its original Q4 2026 target with a transitional period "running into 2027." That framing is now outdated. Travelers and industry readers relying on Greece-focused coverage from earlier in 2026 should treat any reference to a confirmed Q4 2026 ETIAS date as superseded by the July 2026 delay confirmed here.


## The mechanics: what ETIAS will actually require, whenever it launches

Although the launch date has moved, the underlying design of ETIAS has not changed, and it is worth understanding in full since it will eventually apply to most Greece-bound travelers from outside the EU.

**Who needs it.** ETIAS applies to nationals of roughly 59 visa-exempt countries — including the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Japan, and South Korea, among others — who currently enter the Schengen Area and associated countries without a visa for short stays. It does not replace or eliminate visa requirements for nationalities that currently need one; it adds a lighter-touch screening step for nationalities that currently need nothing at all.

**Cost.** The application fee is set at €20 per person, confirmed by a European Commission decision on July 17, 2025 — up from the originally planned €7 written into the 2018 regulation. Applicants under 18 and over 70 are exempt from the fee entirely, as are close family members of EU or EEA citizens exercising free-movement rights, though even exempt applicants must still complete the full application. There is no group or family application; every traveler, including children, requires an individual authorization linked to their own passport.

**What's required to apply.** A machine-readable passport valid for at least three months beyond the planned departure date and issued within the last ten years, a working email address, and a payment card for the fee. The application itself is a short online form covering passport details, personal information, and a set of security and health-related screening questions.

**How long it takes.** Most applications are expected to be approved within minutes of submission. Where additional review is required, decisions are expected within four days; if further documentation is needed, that can extend to 14 days, and in cases requiring an interview, up to 30 days. Once approved, the authorization links electronically to the traveler's passport — there is nothing physical to carry or print.

**How long it lasts.** Once granted, an ETIAS authorization is valid for three years, or until the linked passport expires, whichever comes first — covering multiple trips within that window, each still subject to the existing 90-days-in-any-180-day short-stay limit that already governs visa-exempt Schengen travel today.

## What happens between "launch" and "mandatory": the phased rollout

One detail gets consistently under-communicated in coverage of ETIAS, and it matters enormously for anyone trying to plan around the eventual launch: **ETIAS becoming operational and ETIAS becoming mandatory are two different dates, separated by a substantial grace period.**

Once the system does go live — most likely around April 2027 on current industry estimates, though not yet officially confirmed — a minimum six-month transitional period begins. During that window, the EU has been explicit that travelers who have not yet obtained ETIAS authorization will still be permitted entry, provided they meet all other existing entry conditions (valid passport, sufficient funds, return travel evidence, and so on). The system is designed to encourage voluntary early adoption rather than to penalize travelers for not knowing about a brand-new requirement on day one.

Strict enforcement — the "no ETIAS, no boarding" standard that will eventually mirror how airlines currently check ESTA status before allowing US-bound passengers to board — is not expected to apply until roughly twelve months after the operational launch date. Applying that math to the current best-guess April 2027 launch estimate would place full mandatory enforcement somewhere around early-to-mid 2028, though this remains a projection based on the standard transitional-period design rather than a confirmed date, since the launch date itself is still unofficial.

For practical trip-planning purposes through the remainder of 2026 and into 2027, the safest working assumption is that **no Greece-bound traveler needs to think about ETIAS at all** for any trip currently being booked, and that even once the system does launch, a substantial buffer period will exist before it becomes a hard requirement.


## What Greece-bound travelers should actually do right now

Given everything above, the practical guidance is short. There is no ETIAS application to file, no fee to pay in advance, and no legitimate way to "pre-register" today — any website currently claiming to process ETIAS applications ahead of the official launch is, at best, a paid intermediary charging for a service with no functional product behind it yet, and at worst a scam. The only official source for ETIAS status and launch-date updates is the EU's own portal at travel-europe.europa.eu/etias.

What travelers do need to account for right now, for any 2026 or early-2027 Greek trip, is [the system that is already active](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-ees-biometric-borders-2026): EES biometric registration at the border, with the buffer-time and queue-management guidance that already applies. Beyond that, standard [entry-requirement basics for the relevant nationality](https://greektriplanner.me/blog/greece-travel-requirements-for-us-citizens) — passport validity windows, the 90/180-day short-stay rule, standard travel insurance and documentation — remain unchanged by either EES or the still-pending ETIAS.

Once the eu-LISA board meets in September 2026 and a firmer date emerges, the practical calculus for travelers booking further ahead — particularly for 2027 summer travel, where booking windows for Greece already run [nearly 70% of visitors two or more months ahead of departure](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-top-travel-destination-2026-visa-travel-trends) — may start to matter. Until then, the delay is, for almost everyone planning a Greece trip, simply one less thing to worry about.
