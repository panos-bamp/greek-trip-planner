# The Balkan Road-Trip Boom Reshaping Greek Tourism — and the Data Blackout Hiding Its Real Size

**Subtitle:** Poland just became Halkidiki's third-largest air market. Serbian arrivals grew 65.5% in two years. Romania is smaller than most coverage claims — but pivoting fast to direct flights. And since January 2025, Greece has stopped publishing the one number that would tell us exactly how big its Balkan road-tourism boom really is.

**Excerpt:** While UK, German, and US arrivals to Greece plateau into a "mature phase," a different growth story is unfolding largely outside the headlines: Poland surpassed 1.1 million visitors in 2025, Serbia's arrivals jumped 65.5% in two years, and roughly a third of all foreign visitors to Greece now arrive not by plane but by road, crossing land borders from Bulgaria, North Macedonia, Albania, and beyond. Yet since Bulgaria and Romania joined the Schengen Area for land travel on January 1, 2025, Greece has stopped producing the country-level road-arrival statistics that would let anyone measure this boom precisely. This report reconstructs the clearest available picture — correcting a widely repeated Romania figure along the way — and covers the two-tier way the EU's new biometric border system is now reshaping which Balkan travelers can cross easily, and which cannot.

## Key Takeaways (for Sanity keyTakeaways field)

1. Poland is Greece's cleanest emerging-market growth story: accommodation arrivals surpassed 1.1 million in 2025, up 9.8% cumulatively since 2023, driven by Halkidiki-focused tour operator capacity (TUI Poland, Rainbow Tours, Grecos, Itaka) and new direct air routes, including Aegean Airlines' Thessaloniki–Warsaw service and 33 weekly flights from five Polish cities into Halkidiki for summer 2026.

2. A commonly repeated figure for Romania — 2.5 to 2.7 million visitors, ranked 7th among Greece's source markets — does not match Bank of Greece and INSETE data. The corrected figure, drawn from the Bank of Greece Border Survey, places Romania at roughly 1.44 million visits in 2024, ranked 8th, essentially flat at +0.2% year-on-year, with only about 508,000 of those arriving by air.

3. Since Bulgaria and Romania joined the Schengen Area for land travel on January 1, 2025, routine checks at Greece's northern land borders ended — and with them, the country-level road-arrival statistics that previously let analysts track exactly how many Bulgarian and Romanian travelers crossed into Greece by car each year. No official 2025 country-level road figure exists for either market; only an aggregate national road-growth rate is published.

4. Serbia posted the fastest growth of any of Greece's major tourism markets — arrivals climbed from 426,063 in 2023 to 705,037 in 2025, a 65.5% increase in two years — while Bulgaria remains the single largest land-arrival market, sending an estimated 2.965 million tourist visits in 2024 (roughly 5.3 million raw road crossings, a broader measure that includes transit and repeat-crossing traffic).

5. A significant value gap separates Balkan road tourism from Greece's flight-in markets: Bank of Greece and INSETE data put average per-trip spending at roughly €685–958 for German, British, and American visitors, against a derived €150–350 range for Balkan road tourists — meaning the Balkans supply an estimated third of Greece's total arrivals but only about 12% of tourism receipts.

6. The EU's Entry/Exit System, fully operational since April 10, 2026, has produced a genuine two-tier effect within the Balkan bloc: non-EU nationals (Serbia, North Macedonia, Albania, Turkey) face new biometric queues of 2–3 hours or more at crossings like Evzoni, prompting Greece to suspend daytime EES checks for Serbian citizens from June 1 to August 30, 2026 — while EU members Bulgaria and Romania are entirely unaffected, and their land arrivals have if anything accelerated since Schengen accession.

## ARTICLE BODY

Most coverage of Greek tourism in 2026 tells a story of maturity: the UK, Germany, France, and the US — Greece's five largest markets, together accounting for roughly 49% of all foreign arrivals — are still growing, but slowly, with ELSTAT recording overall arrivals growth easing from 4.2% in 2024 to 2.9% in 2025. That framing is accurate as far as it goes. But it also obscures a second, faster-moving story happening largely at Greece's northern land borders rather than its airports: a genuine Balkan road-tourism boom, led by Poland's clean and consistent growth, Serbia's outright surge, and a structural shift in how Romanian visitors reach the country.

The trouble with telling this story properly is that Greece's own statistical system got harder to read at precisely the moment this boom accelerated. When Bulgaria and Romania became full Schengen members for land travel on January 1, 2025, routine passport checks at those borders ended — which is good news for travelers, but which also quietly eliminated the country-level road-arrival data that used to make Bulgarian and Romanian tourism to Greece measurable with any precision. This report works through what the data does and does not show, corrects a Romania figure that has circulated more widely than the primary sources support, and lays out the very different way 2026's new EU border technology is treating EU-member Balkan travelers versus non-EU ones.

## Poland: the clean growth story

Among Greece's mid-sized source markets, Poland stands out for both the scale and the consistency of its growth. ELSTAT data show Polish accommodation arrivals surpassing 1.1 million in 2025, up a cumulative 9.8% since 2023 — placing Poland alongside the Netherlands (also past the 1-million mark) among the strongest-performing markets outside Greece's traditional top five. The 2024 data point in isolation was even sharper: Poland's arrivals rose 7.3% and nights spent rose 12.6% year-on-year, among the largest increases recorded by any EU market that year.

Northern Greece, and Halkidiki specifically, has become the epicenter of this growth. Grigoris Tasios, president of the Halkidiki Tourism Organisation and Hotel Association, told Tornos News in May 2026 that Poland "recorded 45,000 visitors in 2025, now becoming the third most important source market for air arrivals in Halkidiki," with summer 2026 bringing 33 weekly flight connections from five Polish cities — a scheduling commitment into a domestic Polish travel market of roughly 37 million people. Tasios credited the growth to sustained "advocacy, presence at international exhibitions, and strategic agreements with giants such as TUI, Rainbow, and Grecos." [Halkidiki's broader capacity and investment story is covered in full in our regional statistics report](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/halkidiki-tourism-statistics), where Poland's rise sits alongside a wider pattern of Balkan and Central European demand reshaping the region's visitor mix.

The connectivity backing this growth is substantial and diversifying. Aegean Airlines launched a direct Thessaloniki–Warsaw route, joining existing Ryanair and LOT Polish Airlines service and charter capacity operated by TUI Poland, Rainbow Tours, and Grecos. Notably, this diversification carries some defensive logic: industry reporting has flagged the possibility that Ryanair could close its Thessaloniki base at the end of October, making TUI Poland's expansion and Aegean's new route a meaningful hedge against that risk rather than pure incremental growth. Polish outbound capacity, more broadly, is at record levels — a GTP Headlines analysis of Polish airport data (July 2026) found Greece ranked fourth among outbound destinations from Warsaw Chopin, Kraków, and Łódź combined in summer 2025 (569,645 passengers), with Rhodes the fourth-largest individual charter destination from Warsaw and Heraklion fifth, while Amadeus data showed Poland's total scheduled international capacity up 10.4% year-on-year.

The tour-operator landscape reflects Poland's long, specific relationship with Greek travel: Itaka, one of Poland's largest operators, began its business with charter flights to Greece shortly after 1989, and continues to anchor a package-holiday market — alongside Rainbow Tours, Grecos, and TUI Poland — that favors Crete, Rhodes, Zakynthos, Kos, Corfu, and Halkidiki.


## Romania: correcting the record, and the shift to air

Here the data requires more care, because a figure with real circulation problems needs to be addressed directly. Some secondary tourism analyses — including earlier coverage on this site — have placed Romania 7th among Greece's source markets, with 2.5 to 2.7 million visitors in 2024. Checked against Bank of Greece and INSETE primary data, that figure does not hold up.

INSETE's regional "Who goes where?" report for 2024 (published August 2025), built directly on the Bank of Greece Border Survey, places Romania at approximately **1.44 million visits, ranked 8th** among Greece's source markets — essentially flat, up just 0.2% year-on-year, and behind North Macedonia (7th, roughly 1.59 million). Of that total, only about 508,000 arrived by air in 2024 (ranked 18th by air arrivals specifically, though still up 4.9%), meaning the substantial majority of Romanian visitors reach Greece by driving south through Bulgaria into the north of the country. [We've corrected the corresponding figure in our broader source-market analysis](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-tourism-source-markets) to reflect this Bank of Greece/INSETE data.

That road dependence is not incidental — it is the central fact shaping Romania's Greek tourism story, and it is exactly what 2026's new air connectivity is designed to change. AnimaWings, a Romanian full-service carrier, launched its first-ever year-round Cluj-Napoca–Athens route on April 5, 2026 (twice weekly, Airbus A220-300 aircraft seating 148), followed by Cluj–Thessaloniki from June 2 and Cluj–Zakynthos from June 5, plus additional Thessaloniki service from Iași and Timișoara. Tasios framed the strategic logic in direct terms: "Northern Romania, due to the long distance, could not reach Halkidiki by road. The launch of AnimaWings' direct flights is now enabling us to attract visitors with higher spending power." AnimaWings has stated plans for 57 routes across 13 countries in 2026, including proposed Thessaloniki–Timișoara and Thessaloniki–Bucharest charter services.

The strategic significance extends beyond individual flight routes. On December 3, 2025, Greece, Bulgaria, and Romania signed a tripartite Memorandum of Understanding establishing a Black Sea–Aegean Corridor Platform, aimed at developing cross-border rail and road links connecting Thessaloniki and Alexandroupoli through Bulgaria to Bucharest — infrastructure with direct long-term relevance to Balkan tourism flows, not just freight and trade.

A precise 2025 Romania-to-Greece arrivals figure cannot currently be sourced from published primary data, for reasons explained in the next section — and this report will not manufacture an estimate to fill that gap.


## The data blackout: why Greece stopped counting its own road tourists

This is, in some respects, the most important structural fact in this entire report, and one that receives almost no attention in mainstream coverage of Greek tourism trends. **Since January 1, 2025, when Bulgaria and Romania became full Schengen members for land border travel, Greece has not published country-level statistics for road arrivals from those two countries.**

Before that date, routine passport checks at the Greek-Bulgarian and Greek-Romanian land borders generated a data trail — however imperfect — that let statisticians estimate country-specific road-tourism volumes. Once those checks ended as part of standard Schengen integration, that data trail effectively disappeared. The Bank of Greece continues to estimate aggregate road-arrival growth nationally (reporting, for instance, that road arrivals rose 6.9% in 2025, outpacing air arrivals' 5.6% growth), but it does not — and currently cannot easily — break that aggregate figure down by country of origin for EU-member Balkan states.

Given that Bulgaria alone historically accounted for an estimated 40–45% of Greece's total road arrivals, this is not a minor gap. It means that for two of Greece's most significant Balkan tourism relationships, 2025 is effectively a missing data year at the country level — even as anecdotal and infrastructure evidence (surging Promachonas border-crossing traffic, new Romanian flight routes explicitly designed to serve markets previously reliant on road travel) strongly suggests the underlying volume kept growing. Any Greek tourism analysis — including some circulating elsewhere — that presents a precise 2025 Bulgaria or Romania road-arrival figure should be treated with real skepticism unless it clearly states its methodology and source.

It is also worth being explicit about a second, related measurement problem: Greece's tourism statistics distinguish between "tourist visits" (the Bank of Greece Border Survey and INSETE measure, intended to capture genuine non-resident tourists) and raw "road border crossings" (a police/INSETE measure that also captures transit travelers, repeat crossings by residents of border regions, and immigrants living in Greece returning from trips abroad). Bulgaria illustrates the scale of that gap clearly: an estimated 2.965 million tourist visits in 2024 corresponds to roughly 5.3 million raw recorded road crossings over the same period — nearly double. Any Balkan arrivals figure in this space needs to specify which of these two measures it is using.


## Bulgaria, North Macedonia, and Albania: the rest of the picture

Bulgaria remains Greece's single largest land-arrival market by a clear margin: an estimated 2.965 million tourist visits in 2024 (3rd overall among all source markets, +1.9%), with ELSTAT's 2025 accommodation-arrivals data separately showing Bulgarian arrivals up a further 8.4%. Traffic through the primary Kulata–Promachonas crossing reportedly rose roughly 30% after passport control was abolished at Schengen accession, with more than one million vehicles recorded passing the Greek Promachonas checkpoint since the start of 2025, according to the Serres police union.

North Macedonia functions as both a significant source market and a major transit route, crossing primarily at Bogorodica–Evzoni — the busiest crossing point on the Skopje–Athens motorway corridor — with lower-volume alternatives at Medžitlija–Niki (toward Florina) and Star Dojran–Dojrani. Albania sends substantial flows through the Kakavia crossing (the principal Ioannina–Gjirokastër route) and the secondary Krystallopigi crossing; reported wait times at Kakavia range from a manageable 15–30 minutes off-season to 1–3 hours during peak summer, with the worst congestion consistently reported on Friday evenings (Albanian workers in Greece returning home), Saturday mornings (arriving tourists), and Sunday evenings.

Among 2025 ELSTAT accommodation-arrivals growth rates for the region, Serbia's 11.4% and Bulgaria's 8.4% led the Balkan cohort, with Turkey up 4.8% — a market that grew more than 51% cumulatively between 2023 and 2025, per the earlier GreekReporter/ELSTAT data, finishing just outside Greece's top 10 source markets despite that pace.


## Road versus air, nationally: which is actually growing faster?

For the 2025 full year, the Bank of Greece recorded 37.98 million inbound travelers to Greece (excluding cruise passengers), up 5.6% overall — but broken out by mode of arrival, **road/land-border arrivals grew 6.9%, meaningfully outpacing air arrivals' 5.6% growth.** Non-EU arrivals overall rose a striking 10%, against 2.8% growth for EU arrivals — a gap that reflects, among other things, the strength of non-EU Balkan markets like Serbia and Turkey. Through the first eight months of 2025, the pattern was consistent: road arrivals up 4.8% against air's 4.2%, with August alone showing a dramatic divergence — road arrivals jumped 22.7% that month, against just 2.7% growth via airports.

INSETE's separate air-passenger series, tracking a somewhat different base, recorded roughly 31.0 million international air arrivals in 2025 (+5.9%), with Athens (+9%) and Thessaloniki (+10.2%) both outpacing Greece's regional airports collectively (+3.1%) — itself a signal that gateway cities serving Balkan and Central European connectivity are capturing disproportionate air growth alongside the parallel road-tourism boom. [For the fuller national arrivals and revenue picture underpinning these figures](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-tourism-statistics-2025), 2025 was a genuine record year for Greek tourism overall — but the road-versus-air growth gap documented here is one of the least-covered structural stories inside that headline number.


## The spending gap: volume without matching value

The clearest evidence that Balkan road tourism operates on fundamentally different economics from Greece's flight-in markets comes from spending data. Bank of Greece figures for 2025 show average expenditure per trip of €545.5 (up 2.8% from €530.6 in 2024) and average spend per overnight stay of €96.6 nationally. Broken down by nationality, INSETE data for early 2025 show US visitors spending an average of €958.66 per person — the highest of any nationality measured — with German visitors averaging roughly €685–691 per trip and British visitors €695–731. [The scale of the US market's premium spending is explored further in our dedicated analysis](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/americans-choosing-greece-2026), where American visitors' outsized per-trip spend is a recurring theme across Greek tourism revenue reporting.

Against those figures, derived per-trip spending for Balkan road tourists falls in a markedly lower €150–350 range — a gap wide enough to reshape how Greek tourism receipts break down by market. The practical consequence: the Balkans (Bulgaria, Romania, Serbia, North Macedonia, Albania, and Turkey combined) supply an estimated one-third of all foreign arrivals to Greece, yet contribute only around 12% of total tourism receipts. This is not a story of a market in decline — Halkidiki's hoteliers describe Balkan road tourism as sustaining the region for roughly 110 days of its season — but it is a genuine volume-versus-value tension that shapes investment and marketing decisions across the industry, and helps explain why routes like AnimaWings' new Cluj-Napoca service, explicitly marketed around reaching "visitors with higher spending power," matter disproportionately to how the industry frames Romanian growth going forward.


## EES in 2026: a two-tier border story within the Balkans

The EU's Entry/Exit System, fully operational across the Schengen Area since April 10, 2026, replaced manual passport stamps with biometric fingerprint and facial-scan registration for non-EU travelers. [The national story of EES-related airport queues, Greece's unilateral (and later reversed) UK exemption attempt, and the system's border-flexibility mechanism is covered in full elsewhere on this site](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/greece-fast-tracks-uk-visitors-ees-delay-2026) — but the Balkan land-border dimension of that story deserves its own treatment, because it splits the region in two along an EU-membership line that most coverage misses.

For non-EU Balkan nationals — Serbian, North Macedonian, Albanian, and Turkish citizens specifically — EES has produced real disruption. At the Evzoni crossing, per-traveler processing time rose from roughly 20–30 seconds under the old passport-stamp system to 2–3 minutes for biometric registration, generating queues reported at 2–3 hours or more during peak periods. Greece responded with targeted mitigation at Evzoni and Kipoi (the crossing toward Turkey) — reinforced IT infrastructure, additional booths, traffic rerouting, and extra staffing — following a coordination meeting in Thessaloniki involving regional administrations, police, customs, and municipal authorities. Under the EES flexibility mechanism (Regulation 2025/1534, permitting partial suspension of biometric checks for up to 90 days with a possible 60-day extension), Serbia's Consul General in Thessaloniki, Jelena Vujović Obradović, announced that EES checks for Serbian citizens would be suspended during daytime hours from June 1 through August 30, 2026, with biometric checks retained overnight specifically to avoid the worst queue buildup. Aleksandar Seničić, director of Serbia's national travel-agency association (YUTA), has cited "more than one million Serbian tourists who travel to Greece every summer" as the stakes behind that decision.

For EU-member Bulgaria and Romania, none of this applies. Their citizens are EU nationals, entirely outside EES's scope — and if anything, their land-border experience has improved rather than worsened in 2026, following the removal of routine passport checks altogether at Schengen accession in January 2025. This creates a genuinely two-tier reality within what outside observers often lump together as a single "Balkan road-tourism market": one tier (Bulgaria, Romania) crossing more freely than ever, the other (Serbia, North Macedonia, Albania, Turkey) navigating a brand-new biometric bottleneck at exactly the crossings that matter most to their tourism flow.

## Why now: strategy, connectivity, and the diversification narrative

Greek tourism officials have been explicit, in general terms, about wanting to diversify beyond the country's traditional top markets. Tourism Minister Olga Kefalogianni, describing 2025 as "the best year ever" for Greek tourism, framed the government's 2026 priority as "the qualitative upgrade of the tourism product, the diversification of offerings and the expansion of tourism activity throughout the year and across more regions" (HORECA 2026, February 2026). At the World Beautiful Business Forum in Athens in May 2026, officials reiterated the same themes — market diversification and year-round, regionally balanced tourism — citing new long-haul connectivity, including IndiGo's Mumbai/Delhi–Athens routes launching January 2026, as evidence of the strategy in action.

No official GNTO or SETE document identified in this research names Poland, Romania, or the Balkans specifically as strategic diversification targets in the way, for instance, India or China are sometimes named in long-haul contexts. But the connectivity evidence tells its own story: Aegean's new Warsaw route, AnimaWings' Transylvania-to-Greece network, and December 2025's tripartite Black Sea–Aegean Corridor MoU with Bulgaria and Romania together represent a substantial, concrete pattern of infrastructure investment specifically targeting these markets — strategy expressed through routes and agreements rather than through a single named policy document. [Set against the more mature growth trajectories of Greece's established markets](https://greektriplanner.me/insights/uk-ireland-greece-tourism-2026), where UK arrivals growth of 13.8% over three years already represents strong performance from a much larger base, the Balkan and Central European markets covered here are earlier in their growth curve — which is precisely what makes them the more actionable diversification opportunity for operators, hoteliers, and destination marketers positioning for the next several years rather than the next single season.

For travel businesses and destination marketers reading this as a strategic signal rather than a travel-planning guide: the clearest actionable pattern here is that connectivity investment — specific new routes, not general marketing spend — is what has actually moved these markets, in Poland's case over several years and in Romania's case very recently and rapidly. [Halkidiki's own experience absorbing this demand](https://greektriplanner.me/blog/things-to-do-in-halkidiki), across both its established German package-holiday base and its fast-growing Polish and Balkan visitor mix, offers the clearest single regional case study of what that diversification looks like on the ground.

EU members Bulgaria and Romania are entirely unaffected by EES, since their citizens are EU nationals traveling within the Schengen Area.
